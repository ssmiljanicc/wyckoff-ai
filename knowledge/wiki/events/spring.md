# Spring

## Summary

The **Spring** is the central Phase C shake event in accumulation
structures. It is a downward false break of the [[trading-range]]
support whose purpose is to **transfer stock from weak hands to strong
hands** before the trend move begins
([book p.143](../../../raw/book/pages/page_143.md)).

The book calls Shaking (the event family) "the key event all Wyckoff
traders are waiting for" — no other event "adds greater strength to the
analysis" ([book p.134](../../../raw/book/pages/page_134.md)). It is the
final liquidity grab before the markup begins.

The term **Spring** is short for "springboard". The concept is a
refinement by Robert G. Evans (a Wyckoff student) of Wyckoff's original
**Terminal Shakeout** concept
([book p.143](../../../raw/book/pages/page_143.md)). The distribution-side
mirror is the [[upthrust-after-distribution|UTAD]].

## Key Points

### Preconditions

For a potential spring to be on the table, two things must already have
happened ([book p.134](../../../raw/book/pages/page_134.md)):

1. The previous trend has been stopped (Phase A — climactic or by
   exhaustion).
2. A significant cause has been built (Phase B — the absorption period
   during which the operator has built most of the desired position).

Without these, a probe below support is not a spring; it is just a
continuation of the downtrend.

### What A Spring Does

It is a **false break** of the prior support area
([book p.136](../../../raw/book/pages/page_136.md)). The big traders
absorb the orders triggered by the break — three pools of liquidity
([book p.139–140](../../../raw/book/pages/page_139.md)):

- **Breakout traders** ("the greedy") who see the new low and short
  expecting continuation. Manual traders are one source; automated
  breakout strategies are the **larger** source — the book notes that
  algorithmic momentum strategies trigger at these levels, which is
  why shakes often come with substantial volume.
- **The fearful** — long-held losing positions finally capitulating
  near the lows.
- **The smart-but-early** — traders who anticipated the turn correctly
  but whose stops just got hit.

The big trader exits with profit on the resulting markup, having
absorbed all three counterparty pools.

This is what the book calls the **zero-sum game** mechanic — for the
big trader to position, weak-hand orders must be on the other side. The
shake is necessary because without it, the market cannot move
([book p.135–136](../../../raw/book/pages/page_135.md)).

### Three Graphical Variants

A spring can appear as
([book p.137–138](../../../raw/book/pages/page_137.md)):

1. **One candle (hammer).** A bar that penetrates support and recovers
   essentially the entire move within the same bar, leaving a long
   lower wick. The wick is the rejection signature.
2. **Pattern of 2+ candles.** Same underlying behavior over more time.
   The book is explicit: **the less time the turn takes, the greater
   the strength denoted**
   ([book p.138](../../../raw/book/pages/page_138.md)).
3. **Minor structure.** Price holds in the potential-spring zone long
   enough that a sub-structure forms — a small accumulation that itself
   generates the reversal. This is the methodology being applied
   fractally — see [[concepts/waves-and-fractals]].

### Three Types By Supply Intensity

The book classifies springs by how much supply appears at the break
([book p.144–147](../../../raw/book/pages/page_144.md)):

#### Spring #1 / Terminal Shakeout

Strong supply: **sudden volume increase, range expansion, deep
penetration** below support
([book p.145](../../../raw/book/pages/page_145.md)).

- Spring vs Terminal Shakeout distinction: Spring = shorter moves with
  light or moderate volume; Terminal Shakeout = deeper penetration with
  much higher volume.
- For Spring #1 to succeed, **strong demand must enter** to drive price
  back with wide ranges and elevated volume.
- An early signal of demand return: after penetration, **volume stays
  high but price ranges begin to narrow**.
- If demand does not appear, the price continues down and a new
  accumulation area must be built.

#### Spring #2

Moderate penetration with increase in **both** volume and range
([book p.146](../../../raw/book/pages/page_146.md)).

- A "floating offer" exists — supply is present but not overwhelming.
- The latent supply must be absorbed → expect **successive tests** of
  this zone before the markup.

#### Spring #3

**Supply exhaustion.** Slight penetration with **decrease in volume**
and **narrowing of ranges**
([book p.147](../../../raw/book/pages/page_147.md)).

- Lack of sellers — the cleanest variant.
- Directly tradable — buy positions can be initiated immediately
  without waiting for a separate test (because the lack of volume is
  itself the test).

There is also a **range-internal variant** where the shake stays inside
the structure ([book p.147](../../../raw/book/pages/page_147.md)). The
book notes this denotes greater background strength but is structurally
inferior to a full shake because it cleans up less of the weak-hand
supply.

### Ordinary Shakeout (Reaccumulation Variant)

The **Ordinary Shakeout** is a strong bearish push that occurs **during
an uptrend** (inside a [[structures/reaccumulation|reaccumulation]]),
without the extensive Phase A/B preparation that precedes a textbook
spring ([book p.148](../../../raw/book/pages/page_148.md)).

- Wide price ranges, volume increase (but volume can be high, medium
  or low).
- Same functional role as a spring — liquidity grab before the trend
  resumes — but in a continuation rather than a turn.

### The Spring Test

Except for Spring #3, the spring must be **tested** because the
presence of supply during the break means the upward outcome is not
guaranteed ([book p.149](../../../raw/book/pages/page_149.md)).

A valid test has:

- **Narrowing of ranges.**
- **Decreasing volume.**
- The test stays **above the spring low**.

These together indicate supply exhaustion and confirm the spring as
genuine — a clean buy signal. A test that does not meet these criteria
is "poor quality" and further tests should be expected before
initiating positions.

### Labeling Discipline

The book is strict
([book p.142](../../../raw/book/pages/page_142.md)):

> "Spring must necessarily cause the range to break up. Anything other
> than this should not be labeled as Spring. It will simply be a test."

If the shake does not cause the eventual upward breakout of the
structure, it is by definition not a spring — it is a Phase B test (a
[[st-as-msow|ST as SOW / mSOW]] or just an "ST in B"). See also
[[concepts/labeling-is-last-step]].

### Diagnostics That Favor A Spring Over An Effective Break

Before the shake plays out, two clues raise the probability that a
downside probe is a spring (not a real bearish break)
([book p.141–142](../../../raw/book/pages/page_141.md)):

1. **Phase B contained a UA / mSOS** — the upper-end test denoted
   buyer strength, so the lower-end probe is more likely a fake-out
   (see [[st-as-msos]]).
2. **Price re-enters the range quickly after the break.** Failure to
   stay below denotes strong buyer entry — adds probability that the
   break was false.

## Why It Matters For Wyckoff Reading

- The spring is **the** position-opening event of accumulation. Best
  risk/reward in the structure: stop just below the spring low, target
  the [[concepts/creek-and-ice|creek]] and beyond.
- Three-type classification matters tactically — Spring #3 is directly
  tradable, Spring #1 needs follow-on demand confirmation.
- A spring without a corresponding [[sign-of-strength]] / SOS that
  actually breaks the range is not a spring — discipline the label.
