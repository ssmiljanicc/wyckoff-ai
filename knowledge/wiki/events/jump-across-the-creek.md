# Jump Across the Creek (JAC)

## Summary

**Jump Across the Creek (JAC)** is the metaphorical label for the
**upward break of the [[concepts/creek-and-ice|Creek]]** — the resistance
zone of an accumulation range
([book p.167](../../../raw/book/pages/page_167.md)). The book uses JAC and
[[sign-of-strength|SOS]] effectively as **synonyms** when describing
event #6:

> "Just as the upward rupture movement is labeled as Sign of Strength
> (SOS) or Jump Across the Creek (JAC), the reverse movement confirming
> the rupture is labeled as Last Point of Support (LPS) or Back Up to
> the Edge of the Creek (BUEC)."
> ([book p.167](../../../raw/book/pages/page_167.md))

The metaphor: the Creek is the river of resistance in the range; the
JAC is the trader jumping across it. The follow-up is the
[[back-up-to-the-edge-of-the-creek|BUEC]] — coming back to test the
near bank from the other side.

The distribution mirror is [[fall-through-the-ice|Fall Through The Ice
(FTI)]] / [[sign-of-weakness|SOW]].

## Key Points

### JAC vs SOS — Same Action, Different Vocabulary

The book treats SOS and JAC as alternate names for the same event
([book p.167](../../../raw/book/pages/page_167.md)):

- **SOS (Sign of Strength)** is the **descriptive** label — it names
  the cause (strength of demand) and the diagnostic appearance
  (wide-range, high-volume bullish move).
- **JAC (Jump Across the Creek)** is the **geographic** label — it
  names the location (above the Creek resistance) and frames the move
  as a transition from one structural zone (range) to another (uptrend).

Both terms point to the same bar(s). Most modern Wyckoff writing
prefers SOS; the JAC vocabulary is preserved from Wyckoff's original
metaphors and remains useful for explaining range geography.

### Why The Creek Metaphor Matters

The [[concepts/creek-and-ice|Creek]] is not just a resistance line —
it is the visual / mental model that distinguishes a tactical resistance
(broken often without consequence) from a **structural barrier** (the
zone that, when broken, defines exit from the range). When a trader
labels a move JAC rather than SOS, they emphasize:

- The structural significance of the breached level (this is *the*
  resistance, not just *a* resistance).
- The expectation of a BUEC follow-up (the test from the other side).

### Operational Implication

A JAC is **not the entry**. Same discipline as SOS — see
[[sign-of-strength]]. The entry is the BUEC, where price retests the
Creek from above with low volume and narrow ranges.

A JAC that re-enters the range immediately is by definition not a JAC
— it is an [[upthrust|Upthrust]] (the failed-break case). See
[[failed-signal]].

## Why It Matters For Wyckoff Reading

- JAC and SOS are interchangeable for the **event itself**. The choice
  of label depends on whether the reader emphasizes order-flow
  diagnosis (SOS) or structural geography (JAC).
- The JAC vocabulary makes the BUEC follow-up obvious — "jumping
  across" implies "looking back to see if it's safe".
- If a chart annotation says "JAC" and the chart shows no Creek
  drawn, the annotator is using sloppy vocabulary — the JAC label only
  makes sense in the context of the Creek as a structural zone.

## Cross-Author Readings

### As Used By Bruce Fraser (bruce_fraser)

Fraser keeps the Evans Creek metaphor active: the JAC is the leap through
overhanging supply, with widening price bars and a volume surge as the price
behavior that distinguishes a genuine change from prior range rallies
([Fraser, "Jumping the Creek"](../../../raw/bruce_fraser/posts/articles-wyckoff-2015-06-jumping-the-creek.md)).

In his phase case studies, the JAC/SOS area is part of Phase D only when price
can move toward and out of the accumulation area with better demand quality and
hold above the range on later backups
([Fraser, "Phase Analysis"](../../../raw/bruce_fraser/posts/articles-wyckoff-2016-08-phase-analysis-two-case-studies.md)).
